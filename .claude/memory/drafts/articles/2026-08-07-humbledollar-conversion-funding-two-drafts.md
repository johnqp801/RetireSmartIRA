## DRAFT A: the 17.4% angle

**Title:** Paying 17.4% in a 12% Bracket
**Alternates:** The Careful Conversion / The Cautious One Costs More

MOST ADVICE about Roth conversions quietly assumes a checking account. Convert the money, pay the tax
from cash you already have, leave the IRA alone. It is good advice. It also assumes a balance sheet
that plenty of retirees do not have.

I build software that projects retirement tax bills, and the question I get most often is not whether
to convert. It is what happens when the only money available to pay the tax is sitting inside the
account being converted. So I ran it.

The result that surprised me was not the big number. It was the small one.

**The loop most people know about**

Take the cautious case first. A single retiree, 66, with a million dollars in a traditional IRA and
essentially nothing outside it. Social Security of $28,000 a year. No pension, no brokerage account,
no cash reserve worth the name. Call her Dianne. She is a composite, not a client, and she lives in
Florida, so there is no state income tax muddying anything.

She converts $40,000. Careful, modest, the size people pick when they are trying not to do anything
dramatic.

The tax on that conversion, paid from a checking account she does not have, would be $4,234. So she
withdraws $4,234 from the IRA to cover it. Except that withdrawal is ordinary income too. It generates
tax of its own, which leaves her short again, so she withdraws the shortfall, which creates a little
more tax.

That loop is the gross-up, and it settles quickly. Anyone who has thought about self-funding a
conversion for more than a minute has bumped into it.

What almost nobody mentions is the second loop running inside the first.

**The loop nobody mentions**

How much of your Social Security is taxable depends on your provisional income. The withdrawal Dianne
takes to pay the conversion tax is ordinary income. Ordinary income raises provisional income.
Provisional income decides how much of the benefit gets taxed.

So the act of paying the tax pulls more of her Social Security into taxable income, which raises the
bill, which requires a larger withdrawal.

Here is where it lands. Paying from outside cash, $21,500 of her benefit is taxable. Self-funding,
$23,800 is. An extra $2,300 of Social Security became taxable because of how she paid, not because of
what she converted.

The withdrawal she ends up taking is $5,125. Against the $4,234 she would otherwise have owed, that is
$891 of extra tax on $5,125 withdrawn. Call it 17.4 percent.

Dianne's statutory bracket is 12 percent.

The extra 5.4 points are not a bracket, and nothing on her return will label them. They are her own
benefit being dragged over the line by a withdrawal she took to pay a different tax.

**Why the bold conversion escapes it**

Now the part I found genuinely counterintuitive.

At most 85 percent of a Social Security benefit is ever taxable. That is a statutory ceiling, and once
you are pressed against it, more income cannot drag in anything further.

Run the same woman at $150,000 instead of $40,000 and her benefit is already at the ceiling before the
funding withdrawal happens. The withdrawal drags in nothing extra. The effect that added 5.4 points to
the careful conversion is exactly zero on the aggressive one.

That inverts the intuition. The conversion that feels risky has already absorbed this particular cost.
The conversion that feels safe is the one where the effective rate quietly runs above the bracket you
believe you are in.

I am not suggesting anyone convert more to outrun it. The large conversion carries costs of its own,
and they are bigger. The point is narrower. A small conversion is not a scaled-down version of a large
one. It is a different transaction at a different rate, and the difference appears nowhere on the
return.

**What it costs at the other end**

Since I have the larger figure, here it is. Move Dianne to California and give her the $150,000
conversion, still with no outside cash.

To put $150,000 into a Roth, $219,751 leaves the traditional IRA: the conversion plus $69,751 withdrawn
to pay the tax. That is $1.47 of IRA spent for every dollar arriving in the Roth.

Which sounds worse than it is, and I would rather say so than quote the frightening version. A
conversion is taxable however you pay for it. Of that $69,751, some $44,639 is tax she would have paid
out of a checking account if she had one. It leaves either way. The genuinely additional cost of
self-funding, measured as wealth given up, is $25,111, about 17 percent of the conversion rather than
47.

Still a real number. Just not the one that makes a good headline.

The same conversion in Florida takes $195,469 out of the account instead of $219,751. That entire gap
is state income tax compounding through the gross-up loop.

**The form your custodian hands you**

One more, because it is the error hardest to undo.

Custodians will withhold federal tax from the distribution, and the totals make withholding look
cheap. It is not cheaper. It is a smaller conversion, because dollars withheld never reach the Roth.

At a 24 percent election on Dianne's $40,000 conversion, $9,600 goes to the IRS and $30,400 lands in
the Roth. The tax actually owed was $4,234. She gets the $5,366 of over-withholding back as a refund
next spring. The Roth space does not come back at all. You cannot return later and convert it.

Defaults vary by custodian, and the number on the form is rarely the number you owe. It is worth ten
seconds of attention.

**What I take from it**

None of this settles whether to convert. Leaving money in a traditional IRA has its own bill: required
distributions later, at whatever rates apply then, possibly to a survivor filing single, possibly to
heirs on a ten-year clock.

What it settles is that the cost of self-funding can be worked out before you decide instead of
discovered in April. For Dianne it is 17.4 percent against a 12 percent bracket at $40,000, and
$25,111 at $150,000. For you it is a different number, and it turns on your state, your benefit and
the size you pick.

The arithmetic is not difficult. It is only that nobody shows it to you.

## DRAFT B: the reframe angle

**Title:** Somebody Else's Balance Sheet
**Alternates:** Not Your Question / The Rule That Stops Too Soon

EVERY DISCUSSION of Roth conversions arrives at the same sentence: do not pay the tax out of the IRA
you are converting. It gets repeated so consistently that it has stopped sounding like an argument and
started sounding like a rule.

The advice is right. Paying from outside cash is better, and nothing below disputes that.

What I have started noticing is who it is addressed to. "Pay from outside money" is not guidance for a
retiree whose entire net worth sits in a traditional IRA. It is a description of somebody else's
balance sheet. Said to the wrong person, it does not answer the question. It informs them they are not
the intended audience.

That person is not unusual. A working life of 401(k) deferrals, a rollover at retirement, no
inheritance, no taxable account of consequence. They are the household a conversion window was
practically designed for, and the standard advice hands them a prerequisite instead of an answer.

**The choice they are actually facing**

Someone with $80,000 in a brokerage account is choosing between two ways to pay. Someone without it is
choosing between a self-funded conversion and no conversion at all. Those are not the same question,
and the rule of thumb only answers the first one.

So the useful thing is not to repeat the maxim. It is to price the option the rule dismisses.

Meet Dianne, a composite rather than a client. She is 66, single, retired in California, with
$1,000,000 in a traditional IRA and nothing outside it: no brokerage account, no Roth, no cash reserve
worth naming. Social Security pays her $28,000 a year. She is past 59 and a half, so the early
distribution penalty is not in play, and she is on Medicare.

She wants to convert $150,000.

**What it costs**

Paying from cash, the conversion is clean. The IRA gives up $150,000, the Roth receives $150,000, and
the tax comes from somewhere else.

Funding the tax from the IRA instead, $219,751 leaves the account. The Roth still receives $150,000.
The other $69,751 goes to the IRS and the Franchise Tax Board.

That extra withdrawal exists because the withdrawal is itself taxable. Pull $44,639 to cover a $44,639
bill and you have created more ordinary income, so you owe more than you pulled. Solving that loop is
the gross-up. It converges, and it converges nowhere near the naive figure.

So $1.47 of IRA spent per dollar reaching the Roth. That number invites a conclusion it does not
support, and I would rather correct it myself than let it circulate. A conversion is taxable whichever
pocket pays. Of the $69,751, roughly $44,639 is tax Dianne owes either way. What self-funding actually
costs her, in wealth given up against paying from cash, is $25,111. Around 17 percent of the
conversion, not 47.

Seventeen percent is still enough to change plenty of decisions. It is simply a number you can weigh
rather than a number that ends the conversation.

Geography moves it more than most people expect. The identical conversion in Florida takes $195,469
out of the account rather than $219,751, and the whole difference is state income tax compounding
through that loop. If a move across state lines is anywhere in the plan, the order of operations may
matter more than the size of the conversion.

**Four things that have to be true**

None of that decides anything on its own, because doing nothing is not free either. The money left in
a traditional IRA comes out eventually, under required distributions, at whatever rates apply then,
possibly to a survivor filing single, possibly to heirs facing a ten-year deadline.

When I look at the cases where the expensive version still holds up, four things are true of all of
them.

The rate gap is durable rather than a one-year accident. A two point gap does not survive a
seventeen percent incremental cost. A gap opened by a real low-income window, after retirement and
before Social Security or required distributions begin, can.

There is runway. You are deliberately shrinking the portfolio to change its tax character, and that
trade needs years of tax-free growth to earn back. Late in life, with a short horizon, it usually does
not get them.

Age 59 and a half is cleanly behind you. The converted amount escapes the 10 percent early
distribution penalty. The separate withdrawal taken to pay the tax generally does not, absent an
exception. Under 59 and a half with no outside cash, the numbers rarely work.

And enough is left afterward. Check the balance after the funding withdrawal, not after the
conversion. Dianne's IRA drops to $780,249. Whether that still funds the next thirty years is a
different question from whether the conversion was tax-efficient, and it is the more important one. If
it does not, this stopped being a tax decision.

**One cost that arrives late**

Worth naming because it is easy to miss entirely. In the $150,000 case, the funding withdrawal pushed
Dianne's income into a higher Medicare surcharge tier, from $4,620 to $6,355 in the modeled year.

Nothing about that shows up when she writes the check. Income in 2026 sets Medicare premiums for 2028.
The cost is real, it is caused by how she paid the tax, and it lands two years later in a different
system, long after the decision feels finished.

**Restating the rule**

Pay from outside cash if you have it. That was never in dispute.

But "never fund it from the IRA" stops exactly where the hard cases start, and the households most
likely to benefit from a conversion window are frequently the ones with no cash to pay for it. Told to
Dianne, the rule is not advice. It is a description of a situation she is not in.

Her real choice is a smaller self-funded conversion, sized so the funding withdrawal does not cross a
Medicare tier or push her benefit to its taxable ceiling, weighed against not converting at all.
Sometimes the answer is still no. It ought to be reached by arithmetic rather than by repeating the
maxim at her.
