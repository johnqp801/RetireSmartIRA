## Part 1 — Six engine/UX fixes (all merged, all shipped in 63 except the last)

Order was forced, not arbitrary: the Pub 915 bug made taxable SS discontinuous at the second threshold, and folding a discontinuous function into the gross-up fixed point produces genuine limit cycles.

### 1. `1f006a0` Pub 915 50%-tier

`TaxCalculationEngine.swift:834`: `min(excessOverFirst, ss*0.5)` → `min(excessOverFirst * 0.5, ss*0.5)`. IRC §86(a)(1) takes the lesser of one-half of benefits or **one-half of the excess**; limb (B) was missing its halving. Overstated taxable benefits up to 2x for single provisional income $25k-$34k, MFJ $32k-$44k.

**Three independent confirmations**, not one reading: the adjacent 85% branch already halves its band; the existing 85%-tier tests spell out Pub 915 line 14 with the same halving; and an un-halved excess taxes benefits at a **full dollar per dollar**, a rate no tier of §86 provides. A property test caught exactly that (`delta 250.0 <= 212.51` failed).

**Two tests pinned the wrong value** and were corrected with worksheet arithmetic in the comments: `RetireSmartIRATests.swift:206` ($2,000 → $1,000) and `VADisabilityTests.swift:88` ($5,000 → $2,500).

New `SocialSecurityPub915Tests` (7 tests) adds statutory cases plus domain-wide properties: non-decreasing in other income, rises at most 85 cents per dollar, never exceeds 85% of benefits.

### 2. `6247f01` Gross-up convergence

The Picard fixed point stopped after 3 passes. Measured unfunded tax on **solvent** households: FL $600K → $7; CA $300K → $4,131; MN $600K → $11,092; **CA $600K → $13,894** (prior note said worst was $8,344). That shortfall is tax reported but never removed from any account, so ending balances were overstated by the full amount.

**Perf was measured, not assumed.** Naively raising the budget to 40 cost **37%** on the hot path (MFJ 35-year persona 11.44s → 15.67s against a 15s budget), because the loop runs per year per optimizer candidate.

**Fixed with Aitken delta-squared** after two Picard steps. The tax map is piecewise LINEAR in `dW`, so between bracket boundaries the extrapolation is *exact*. Converges in one round on two evaluations, fewer than the old three. Same persona now **11.53s**, indistinguishable from baseline, converging to under a penny.

One test rebaselined: `InfeasibleYearEngineTests` had asserted this exact scenario carried MORE than $1 of residue so the gate had to look past it. That scenario was leaving $4,131 unfunded.

### 3. `59f8f04` Social Security recursion through the gross-up

`taxableSS` was computed once pre-Step-7 and never revisited, so the tax-funding withdrawal's own effect on benefit taxation was dropped. Now a closure over `otherIncomeForSSTax + dW + saleGain`, evaluated INSIDE the fixed point.

Understatement measured (single, 70, whole tax funded from traditional): FL $7,222 taxable SS / $2,037 tax; CA $9,038/$2,762; VT $11,888/$4,762; **MN $11,888/$4,809**. Zero on large conversions (benefits already at the 85% ceiling), which is why no reference scenario moved.

**The MAGI trap, handled:** ACA MAGI = AGI + non-taxable SS + tax-exempt is algebraically invariant to the taxable/non-taxable split, so `reportedMagiAddback` falls by exactly what `reportedAGI` rises by. Verified across a 36-profile grid that reported MAGI moves by exactly the change in the withdrawal and nothing else.

### 4. `c5d3fcc` Cash-flow tax funding + feasibility copy

Resolved the Roth/HSA-only false-infeasibility. **Reproduced first, and it was worse than the prior note recorded:** $30,000 pension, **ZERO expenses**, $2M Roth, $585 tax, every year flagged "cannot pay its modeled tax" while $30,000 of unspent income evaporated annually. Confirmed expenses have zero effect on the gate (probes at $60k/$0/$40k gave byte-identical shortfalls).

**Sharpest statement:** Roth was already an acceptable source for GROCERIES but not for TAX. At $60k expenses the engine drained Roth by exactly $30,000 for living costs in the same year it declared $585 of tax unfundable.

Fix: unspent income counts toward funding the year's tax. **Measurement only, never a source that moves a balance** (the surplus was already discarded, so there is nothing to debit). Ending positions untouched, pinned by a test. Because the gate also requires `assetsExhausted`, only already-flagged years can change.

Does NOT extend the cascade to Roth. A household whose income falls short of expenses is still flagged, now with honest copy.

**Copy rewritten** (John chose "model-limit, neutral" of 3 options). Banner was claiming the totals "do not describe an outcome this household can reach" and balances "the household could never have held". Now: *"Tax funding not modeled in N years."* Two tests pin the ABSENCE of the old accusation by phrase.

### 5. `8a8f481` IRMAA MAGI vs ACA MAGI split + cliff-buffer repair

Different statutory quantities differing by exactly the untaxed half of benefits: IRMAA (42 U.S.C. §1395r(i)(4)) has **NO** benefit add-back; ACA (IRC §36B(d)(2)(B)) does. One add-back was used for both, so IRMAA MAGI was overstated by **$20,704** at a $3,000/mo benefit, $19,472 at $4,000/mo. Not display-only: the optimizer reads it via `.limitToIRMAA`.

**The decisive corroboration was the codebase disagreeing with itself:** `DataManager.irmaaMagi` (single-year) was already correct at `estimatedAGI + taxExemptInterestTotal`, while its ACA MAGI added `nonTaxableSS`. Two engines, two definitions of the same thing.

**The latent bug this exposed.** Optimizer cliff candidates assume converting $X raises IRMAA MAGI by $X. That held ONLY because the wrong add-back cancelled the benefit-taxation feedback exactly. Corrected, a conversion also drags benefits into taxation, so the candidate overshoots into the `cliffBuffer` dead zone it was aiming at. MFJ reference profile year 2031 landed $213,471 against a $213,001 target. The objective cannot see this: the zone sits below the threshold, so the margin scores as free.

**Fixed by post-selection repair, and a per-candidate preference was tried and REJECTED on evidence:** preferring non-overshooting candidates during the sweep changes which amount wins, which changes the next iteration's baseline, so the greedy stops converging. It cost a full extra fixed-point pass and blew `MultiYearStrategyManager`'s 5s compute deadline. The repair costs at most 3 projections per year and only on affected years: manager suite 4.136s vs a 4.111s baseline.

### 6. `2d89141` TAXSIM 50%-band oracle coverage

**All 20 existing TAXSIM scenarios with Social Security land in the 85% tier.** Not one was in the 50% band, so the differential oracle was structurally blind to the tier the Pub 915 bug lived in.

Six scenarios added (FL/CA/CO/NY/PA/NC, single + MFJ), fetched from taxsim.nber.org with John's authorization.

**Key lesson: being in the band is NOT sufficient.** Low provisional income usually means the standard deduction zeroes the tax. Of six as first drafted, only ONE moved federal tax past the $200 tolerance and two returned $0 either way. Resized so the excess is roughly half of gross benefits, where the error peaks, with two filers under 65.

**Verified by reintroducing the bug:** oracle drops to 21/26 with deltas +436/+360/+528/+530/+580. Three independent sources agree: hand-computed Pub 915 (930/1118/1316/580/730), TAXSIM (940/1118/1328/600/750), engine matches TAXSIM 26/26.

### The verification detour, and a mistake worth remembering

I reported the article's Household A figures as wrong (engine $56,776 vs published ~$53,900) and framed it as "the article is stale." **John pushed back: "when we wrote the article we used the tax engine."** He was right and I was wrong.

Root cause: the web draft described Household A as "$360,000, including about $70,000 of qualified dividends and long-term gains" and never mentioned its **$80,000 of Social Security**. The verification modeled the other $290,000 as pension and taxed all of it. Only 85% of a benefit is taxable, so $80,000 of SS keeps **$12,000** out of taxable income; at 24% marginal that is exactly the $2,876 gap. AGI is $348,000, matching the July 11 note's "MAGI $348k".

A diagnostic agent then found that adding $12,000 of *deduction* also reproduces the number and concluded the article had wrongly applied an unphased senior bonus. Right arithmetic, wrong mechanism. I let a numerical coincidence override the July 11 note's explicit statement that the bonus is "gone at $250k joint".

**I also byte-verified the engine had not changed:** `calculateFederalTax` and the senior-bonus math identical since 2026-07-11, `tax-2026.json` and `TaxYearConfig` untouched.

Result: `RetireSmartIRATests/WidowTaxArticleScenarioTests.swift` (`46855fa`, 11 tests) now pins all six profiles. ## Process lessons

**The cwd trap bit four times.** The Bash shell resets its working directory between calls, so memory commits landed on `root-workspace` instead of `main` twice, a release-notes commit did the same, and twice I misdiagnosed a file as missing because a relative-path check ran from the root repo. Every one required a cherry-pick or a retraction. **Use `git -C <abs-path>` for every git write and every existence check.**

**A denied Bash call is not proof nothing happened.** The first `git push origin main` returned a permission denial, and I told John it had not gone out. It had. `git ls-remote` against the live remote is the only trustworthy check, not the cached remote-tracking ref and not the tool's own error.

**Verify test non-vacuity, not just green.** The TAXSIM fixtures needed a deliberate bug-reintroduction to prove they discriminate; the audit gate needed a profile-count check to prove it had not skipped; the widow-tax suite needed the 85%-ceiling break to prove it had teeth. Green alone means little on a new test.

**Subject:** 2.3 is live, ahead of the date I gave you

Hi Joan,

A few days ago I told you the selectable tax payment source would land in early August. It went live today on iPhone, iPad, and Mac.

Here's what's in it that you specifically raised.

**You now choose how the conversion tax gets paid.** Three options: have it withheld from the conversion itself, pay it from your other accounts, or pay it from money outside the plan. Existing plans keep behaving exactly as they did, so nothing you've already built changes unless you change it. For your situation, where there isn't outside cash sitting idle, the second option is what the app was already doing quietly. Now it says so, and you can see what each choice leaves in the Roth.

**The Social Security gap is closed.** This is the item I told you was on my list. When the plan takes an extra withdrawal to cover conversion tax, that withdrawal is ordinary income, so it can pull more of your benefit into taxable territory. The app was working out your taxable benefit before that withdrawal and never revisiting it. It now folds back in, the way it actually works on a return.

**Years the plan cannot fund are flagged.** If a year's tax bill is bigger than your taxable and IRA balances can cover, that year is marked with the reason, and there's a count at the top of the plan. This is the case you described. It's a new check rather than a change to the math, so a plan you built earlier might start showing a warning it didn't before. Those years were never fundable; the app simply wasn't surfacing it.

**The tradeoff is now stated plainly.** Paying conversion tax out of the IRA leaves less in the Roth than paying it from outside money. That's the Ed Slott concern, and the app now says so where you make the choice, instead of leaving you to work it out.

One follow-up on your modeling question: you don't need three separate inputs. A single multi-year run starting in 2027, with your own claim age set to 70, handles your benefit correctly alongside your husband's. The only split worth making is the one you already had right, this year in the single-year model while you still have earned income, then a fresh multi-year plan from 2027.

Thanks for writing in, and for pushing on that warning message until it made sense. That exchange is a good part of why this release looks the way it does.
